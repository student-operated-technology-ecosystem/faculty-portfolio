07 · ASSESSMENT

Involvement in Assessment

Assessment is embedded in my curriculum design through explicit outcome mapping, observable student artifacts, and iterative revision of learning experiences.

Assessment Through Curriculum Mapping

My approach begins by identifying what students are expected to know and do, then designing learning experiences that produce observable evidence of those outcomes. In CIT-205, the current curriculum redesign maps course outcomes into a ten-pillar instructional sequence and connects Field Briefs, missions, concept checks, and operational artifacts to those expectations.

This makes assessment part of the course architecture rather than an activity added at the end of instruction.

Multiple Forms of Evidence

CIT-205 uses several forms of assessment because help-desk readiness requires more than factual recall. Vocabulary and concept checks measure foundational knowledge; troubleshooting scenarios expose student reasoning; validation and documentation assignments demonstrate technical practice; ticket and knowledge-base work assess communication and process; and the ACE Qualification Practicum asks students to integrate those skills.

Assessment sequence
Know it → reason with it → perform it → document it → integrate it.

Authentic Artifacts

Applied assignments produce artifacts that can be reviewed as evidence of learning. These include workstation and server validation records, troubleshooting decisions, tickets, knowledge-base articles, GitHub documentation contributions, and qualification work. The artifact is not simply proof that an assignment was submitted; it shows how the student observed, reasoned, communicated, and documented technical work.

Feedback Into Course Design

Assessment evidence also informs revision. When students struggle with terminology, process, documentation, or transfer between concepts and operational work, the instructional response can be targeted to that point in the sequence. Field Briefs, scenarios, missions, and supporting materials can then be revised without disconnecting them from the underlying course outcomes.

This creates a feedback loop in which assessment informs curriculum improvement rather than functioning only as a mechanism for assigning grades.

Broader Assessment & Research Context

My work on the NSF Future of Work project with Carnegie Mellon University and CCAC has also involved curriculum mapping and learning-science approaches to community-college information technology education. The resulting 2024 AERA paper, Community College Information Technology Education: Curriculum Mapping, a Learning Science Framework, and AI Learning Technologies, reflects that broader connection between curriculum structure, learning evidence, and instructional technology.
