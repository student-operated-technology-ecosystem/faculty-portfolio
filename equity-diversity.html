08 · EQUITY & DIVERSITY

Accessible & Inclusive Technical Learning

My approach to equity in technical education focuses on reducing unnecessary barriers to participation while maintaining meaningful technical expectations.

Access Without Lowering the Standard

Students enter technology courses with different levels of prior experience, confidence, available time, and familiarity with professional tools. Course design should make the path into the work understandable without reducing the level of work students are ultimately expected to perform.

In practice, this means making expectations visible, allowing students multiple opportunities to develop mastery, and building enough instructional support around authentic technical work that prior exposure is not mistaken for ability.

Design principle
Reduce barriers to learning, not the expectations for learning.

Accessible Course Materials

Accessibility is treated as part of course construction rather than a retrofit. Blackboard materials are reviewed with Ally, and course documents and instructional resources are revised to improve accessibility, readability, structure, and navigation. The goal is for students to encounter usable materials before an accessibility problem becomes an individual student's burden to solve.

Flexible Paths to Mastery

My courses emphasize demonstrated learning over a single attempt under a single set of conditions. Students have flexibility in how they progress toward mastery, including opportunities to revisit assessments and seek additional support. This approach keeps the learning target intact while reducing the extent to which timing, test-taking speed, or one unsuccessful attempt determines the student's opportunity to demonstrate understanding.

Belonging Through Authentic Roles

Applied technical environments can give students a meaningful role before they see themselves as established technology professionals. In CIT-205 and the Student-Operated Technology Ecosystem, students participate as contributors to support, documentation, validation, knowledge, and infrastructure workflows. Their work can produce something useful to the next student or technician rather than existing only for a grade.

This model creates multiple ways to contribute. Technical troubleshooting matters, but so do documentation, communication, organization, review, customer support, and careful operational practice.

Student Interests as an Entry Point

Where appropriate, I use student interests and career goals as context for technical work without changing the underlying competency being assessed. A networking, support, documentation, or cybersecurity objective remains the same, but the operational setting can become more relevant to the student completing it.

This allows personalization to function as an invitation into rigorous technical work rather than as a substitute for it.

Broader Access to Technical Careers

My work with Carnegie Mellon University and CCAC on the NSF Future of Work project focused in part on accelerating learning and broadening access to digital-economy careers through curriculum alignment, learning science, and AI-augmented educational technologies. That work complements the same goal at the course level: create clearer pathways into demanding technical learning and give more students a credible opportunity to succeed in it.

Selected Evidence

  • Blackboard Ally accessibility review and remediation of course materials
  • Flexible assessment and opportunities to demonstrate mastery
  • Structured Field Briefs and applied missions that make expectations explicit
  • Multiple operational roles within CIT-205 and SOTE
  • Student-interest contextualization while preserving common competencies
  • NSF / Carnegie Mellon University work addressing access to digital-economy learning and careers
